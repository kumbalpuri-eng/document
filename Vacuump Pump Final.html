How It Works

Single-slider rotary vane vacuum pump — 4-stage operating cycle

Slider rotates continuously — each revolution completes one full Induction → Isolation → Compression → Exhaust cycle
1

Induction

As the eccentric rotor turns, the single slider passes the inlet port at the top. The chamber between the slider and the housing wall expands because the rotor moves away from the housing in this region. This expanding volume creates negative pressure (vacuum), drawing atmospheric air in through the inlet port.

2

Isolation

After passing the inlet, the slider seals off the chamber. The trapped air is now completely enclosed between the slider, the housing wall, the rotor surface, and the end plates. No air can enter or escape — the chamber is fully isolated from both inlet and exhaust.

3

Compression

Continued rotation moves the slider into a region where the eccentric rotor approaches the housing wall. The isolated chamber volume progressively decreases, compressing the trapped air. Engine oil fills the gap between the slider tip and housing wall, ensuring an airtight seal.

4

Exhaust

When the slider reaches the exhaust port, the compressed air is pushed out through a one-way check valve. The check valve prevents backflow. Oil carried with the exhaust air is separated in the vehicle's oil system. The cycle repeats continuously with each revolution.

Plant Head Q&A

Key questions and answers

Q: Why is Station 85 the biggest problem?
Station 85 handles rotor, vane, and cover assembly with screwing (7-8 Nm) and camera inspection. It accounts for 39.9% of total downtime (42.5h) across 75 events. Root causes are screwing gun failures, poka-yoke sensor issues, and oil supply interruptions. 8 of the top 10 worst breakdowns originated here.
Q: What is the bottleneck and can we fix it?
Station 90 (Leakage Test at 1 bar, 90s) is the line bottleneck with the longest cycle time. Since it is a test station, reducing cycle time requires process engineering — possibly parallel test chambers or optimized pressure stabilization. This is a capital-intensive change with long lead time.
Q: How dependent are we on a single customer?
100% revenue comes from Ford Sanand. This is a critical concentration risk. Any disruption at Ford (shutdown, model change, volume reduction) directly impacts our entire line. Mitigation: actively pursuing alternate OEM customers for similar vacuum pump platforms.
Q: What is the status of Station 70 spike in July?
Station 70 (Spring/Valve Assembly) had 29 events in July alone, primarily air leakage issues. This is a new emerging hotspot. Emergency review initiated. Suspected cause: batch-level quality issue from Kern Liebers (Germany) spring supplier. IQC re-inspection of incoming stock underway.
Q: What is our OEE and is it meeting target?
Target OEE is 85% (A:92% x P:95% x Q:98%). Current OEE is likely below target due to the 106.6h downtime over 7 months. The biggest drag is Availability — Station 85 alone drops it significantly. If we cut St.85 downtime by 40% (~17h), we recover approximately 1.5% OEE.
Q: What supply chain risks keep you awake at night?
Three things: (1) Rockman Industries housing porosity — 100% imported from India but still has quality escapes. (2) Kern Liebers springs from Germany — long lead time, no local alternative, and now showing quality issues. (3) Single-customer dependency — if Ford Sanand stops for even one day, our entire production halts with no buffer orders.

Introduction to Classification

01 / 35

Machine Learning enables computers to learn patterns from data and make predictions.

One of the most common predictive tasks is classification.

Classification assigns observations into predefined categories based on input features.

Examples
  • Email → Spam or Not Spam
  • Loan Application → Approved or Rejected
  • Customer → Will Buy or Not Buy

Classification models help organisations make data-driven decisions across industries.

Evaluating Classification Models

08 / 35

After training a model we must evaluate its performance.

Key metrics:

  • Accuracy
  • Precision
  • Recall
  • F1 Score
  • ROC Curve and AUC

Different metrics help evaluate different types of errors.

Accuracy

09 / 35

Accuracy measures the proportion of correct predictions.

Accuracy = Correct / Total
Example
  • 100 loan predictions
  • Correct predictions = 92
  • Accuracy = 92%

However accuracy can be misleading with imbalanced data.

Precision

10 / 35

Precision measures the correctness of positive predictions.

Precision = TP / (TP + FP)
Fraud Detection Example
  • Model predicts 20 fraud cases
  • Only 15 are actual fraud
  • Precision = 15/20 = 75%

High precision reduces false alarms.

Recall (Sensitivity)

11 / 35

Recall measures how many actual positives are correctly detected.

Recall = TP / (TP + FN)
Medical Diagnosis Example
  • 100 patients have disease
  • Model detects 90
  • Recall = 90%

High recall reduces missed cases.

F1 Score

12 / 35

F1 Score combines precision and recall.

It is the harmonic mean of precision and recall.

F1 Score is useful when datasets are imbalanced.

A good F1 score indicates balanced model performance.

ROC Curve and AUC

13 / 35

ROC curve shows tradeoff between:

  • True Positive Rate
  • False Positive Rate

The Area Under Curve (AUC) measures model performance.

AUC Values
1.0
Perfect
0.5
Random

Introduction to Decision Trees

14 / 35

Decision trees are one of the most intuitive machine learning models.

They represent decisions using a tree structure.

  • Each node represents a test on a feature
  • Each branch represents an outcome
  • Leaf nodes represent predictions

Decision trees are easy to interpret and visualize.

Structure of a Decision Tree

15 / 35

A decision tree contains three main components.

Root Node

Represents the entire dataset

Internal Nodes

Decision points that split the data

Leaf Nodes

Final prediction or class label

How Decision Trees Work

16 / 35

Decision trees work by recursively splitting data.

Goal: Create subsets where observations are similar.

Each split selects the feature that best separates classes.

Example Decision Path

Income > 50K ?

Yes → Approved

No → Credit Score Check

Decision Tree Example

17 / 35

Predict whether a customer will buy a product.

Decision Rules
  • Age < 30
  • Income > 40k
  • Previous Purchase = Yes
Decision Tree Path

Age < 30 → Yes

Income > 40k → Yes

Prediction → Buy Product

How Trees Decide the Best Split

18 / 35

Decision trees choose splits using impurity measures.

Two common criteria:

Gini Impurity

Entropy

Goal: Reduce impurity in each node.

The more homogeneous the node, the better the split.

Gini Impurity

19 / 35

Gini impurity measures how often a randomly chosen element would be misclassified.

Properties:

Gini = 0

Perfect purity

Higher value

Mixed classes

Lower Gini means better split.

Decision trees select the split that minimizes Gini impurity.

Entropy

20 / 35

Entropy measures disorder or uncertainty.

High entropy

Mixed classes

Low entropy

Pure node

Decision trees aim to minimize entropy.

Information Gain

Reduction in entropy after split.

Problem with Decision Trees

21 / 35

Decision trees are powerful but have a limitation.

They can overfit the training data

Overfitting occurs when the tree becomes very deep and learns noise in the dataset.

Result: Poor performance on new data.

Pruning Decision Trees

22 / 35

Pruning reduces tree complexity and prevents overfitting.

Two types:

Pre-Pruning

Stop tree growth early.

Example

Limit maximum depth

Post-Pruning

Grow full tree then remove unnecessary branches.

Limitations of Single Decision Trees

24 / 35

Problems with single trees:

  • Overfitting
  • Sensitive to small data changes
  • High variance

Solution: Ensemble learning methods.

The most popular method is Random Forest.

Introduction to Random Forest

25 / 35

Random Forest is an ensemble machine learning algorithm.

It combines multiple decision trees.

  • Each tree makes a prediction
  • Final prediction via majority voting

Result: More accurate and robust model.

How Random Forest Works

26 / 35

Random forest uses two main techniques:

Bagging (Bootstrap Sampling)

Random Feature Selection

These introduce randomness and reduce overfitting.

Bagging (Bootstrap Aggregation)

27 / 35

Each tree is trained on a random subset of data.

Sampling is done with replacement.

This creates diverse training datasets.

Benefit: Reduces variance and improves stability.

Random Feature Selection

28 / 35

At each split only a random subset of features is considered.

This ensures trees are different from each other.

Benefit: Reduces correlation among trees. Improves overall prediction accuracy.

Advantages of Random Forest

29 / 35
High prediction accuracy
Less overfitting than single trees
Robust to noise and outliers
Works with large datasets
Handles many input features

Limitations of Random Forest

30 / 35
  • Less interpretable than decision trees
  • Higher computational cost
  • Hard to visualize entire model

Still widely used because of strong predictive power.
